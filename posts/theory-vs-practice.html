Theory vs. Practice

Why Ideas and Theories Matter in a World That Prioritizes Execution

Diop Papa Makhtar

·
·
Just now

--

Throughout my life, I have always been drawn to ideas and have found solace in proven theories. The rest, I’ve believed, takes care of itself. Mathematics has been my preferred domain — not because I was particularly good at it, but because I loved its abstract nature.

However, during my undergraduate years, I became increasingly disconnected from this abstract beauty. The world around me, particularly the educational and industrial systems, was heavily pushing young people like me toward practical fields — applied sciences, engineering, and other disciplines that were considered “useful” because they could be directly applied in factories and offices.

This trend wasn’t just about education; it was a reflection of a broader societal shift. In many countries, including mine, the emphasis was on creating a workforce ready to fill the roles that would drive the economy — roles that required practical skills and hands-on experience rather than theoretical knowledge. This shift led to the gradual abandonment of fields filled with theories that needed to be learned, understood, and questioned.

What I aim to discuss in this article is not just my personal journey but the broader implications of this trend. There is a pervasive, and I believe misguided, mantra that “practice is better than theory” and “execution is better than ideas.” But the reality is, there is no practice without theory, nor execution without ideas. In the following paragraphs, I’ll explore the importance of theories and ideas, particularly in the realms of tech entrepreneurship and mathematics, and why they should not be undervalued.

The Disconnect from Abstract Mathematics

Even after I left the abstract part of mathematics behind, it never really left me. I continued to engage with it in my own way, picking up mathematics books from time to time to stay informed about the field’s evolution and the emergence of new theories. These occasional readings kept me connected to the field, and over time, I found myself particularly drawn to dynamical systems — a branch of mathematics that closely relates to the mechanical engineering field I studied.

What fascinated me most about dynamical systems was their ability to describe the world around us — a world full of variations and changes. Within this field, the subfield of “ill-posed problems” caught my attention. These are problems where a small change in initial conditions can lead to vastly different outcomes, which is particularly intriguing because it mirrors so much of what we see in the real world.

This subfield resonated with me not just because of its mathematical elegance, but because of its applicability to digital virality — an area I was increasingly interested in. In today’s world, platforms like social media and online marketplaces are driven by ranking and recommendation engines that rely on mathematical formulas. These formulas, in many ways, function like dynamical systems. I began to wonder: could there be parts of these algorithms that are ill-posed? Could small changes in how a piece of content is designed or distributed lead to it going viral in ways that are unpredictable and uncontrollable?

For instance, imagine a social media post that garners billions of views simply because of a slight tweak in its design or the timing of its release. If we could understand and tame the ill-posed parts of these algorithms, it might be possible to predict — or even design — such virality.

The Challenges of Pursuing Theoretical Exploration

Of course, this is easier said than done. The quest I’ve described above is not just about knowing the theory — it also requires access to vast amounts of data and the computational power to experiment with it. Fortunately, some digital platforms have begun sharing anonymized data that independent researchers like myself can use. But the computational aspect remains a significant challenge. Running experiments of this nature on a simple laptop is nearly impossible.

This article might sound like a plea for more computational resources, but that’s not its primary purpose. My goal is to convince you that ideas and theories matter. If your work is solely based on doing, don’t be surprised if one day you’re replaced by an AI that can do what you do — but faster, better, and at a lower cost. The real edge lies in understanding the theories behind the practical tasks, in knowing how to mix and match these theories with new ideas to create better solutions.

The Importance of Theories in Innovation

Whenever I find myself advocating for the importance of theories, I’m reminded of the story of the engineer at Ford who conceived the idea of the assembly line — a concept that revolutionized not just the company, but the entire automotive industry. If this engineer had spent his days just doing his job at Ford, he might never have come up with this groundbreaking idea. Instead, he went to observe another industry, spent hours watching how people in a completely different field worked, and in doing so, came up with the idea that shaped the Ford Motor Company we know today.

What we often overlook is that when we observe something, our minds are constantly building theories about the “how,” the “why,” the “when,” and so on. These theories are then mixed with our goals, our past experiences, and our aspirations to give us possible paths to follow. The little I know about cognitive science tells me that this is how our minds work. So, instead of just doing the same thing over and over, look for other things that might help you do your work better. Engage with theories. Explore new ideas. You never know where they might lead you.

In the rush to prioritize practice and execution, we must not forget the importance of ideas and theories. They are the foundation upon which practices are built and the spark that ignites meaningful innovation. Without them, we risk becoming mere cogs in a machine — replaceable and devoid of creativity. So, let’s not undervalue the abstract in favor of the practical. Theories and ideas matter, and they always will.
